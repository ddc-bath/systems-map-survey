Introduction

For more than two decades, digital inequalities have remained stubbornly resistant to change despite concerted efforts to tackle them. People from lower socioeconomic groups, ethnic minorities, older age groups, and those who are geographically disadvantaged remain less likely to engage with and benefit from digital technologies.

Whilst there has been a justifiable focus on digital exclusion, the reasons why inequalities persist are multifaceted — rooted in the systems and structures of everyday life rather than in individual failings. Building on existing work on access and inclusion, and exploring the wider systems through which inequalities are produced and maintained, we can begin to understand not just who is excluded, but why — and why.

🗺️ About this project

This project uses systems thinking to map the forces, drivers, and interacting parts from which digital inequalities emerge. The Digital Divide Collective (Bath), in collaboration with researchers from the University of Bath, ran a series of workshops in 2025. Workshop outputs were analysed by researchers, and eight themes were identified: Access and Inclusion; Infrastructure; Policy and Regulation; Trust, Safety and Protection; Skills, Learning and Ongoing Support; Design, Purpose and Values; Community, Place and Local Leadership; and Socioeconomic Issues.

From these, the feedback loops that perpetuate digital inequalities were mapped, grouped by socioecological level — from motivation, skills, and trust at the individual level, to policy, design, infrastructure, and commercial forces at the societal level.

The Digital Divide Systems Map is not the finished product. We now welcome your constructive critique. What works? What doesn't? What have we missed? Systems maps develop iteratively, in dialogue with those closest to the issues. Please complete the short questionnaire at the end of this document and share your thoughts.

How to Read the Systems Map

Before looking at the map, it helps to understand how it works. The map is built from feedback loops — groups of connected variables that influence each other — linked together by connections between loops, and shaped by external forces.

🔄 Feedback loops

A feedback loop shows how variables within a system are connected. When one variable changes, it causes a change — up or down — in another. This is shown visually by arrows:

Positive (blue) Negative (red)

For example, in the Trust loop (Figure 1): as Trust in digital increases, so does Willingness to engage — a positive (blue) arrow. But as Quality of experience goes down, Perceived security risks go up — a negative (red) arrow.

Loops are either reinforcing (things get progressively better or worse) or balancing (variables counteract each other, keeping things roughly stable). Trust is an example of a balancing loop.

Figure 1 — The Digital Trust feedback loop, showing positive (blue) and negative (red) arrows

Connections between loops

As well as the feedback loops themselves, there are connections between loops shown by grey connectors. For example, Trust in digital is influenced by Trust in institutions (from the Policy loop) and by Digital Exclusion (from the Design loop). It then influences Digital Skills/Literacy in the Skills loop.

These cross-loop connections are important: they show how a change in one part of the system can ripple outwards into others.

External variables

Every system is shaped by forces that lie outside its boundary — things that influence the system but are not themselves influenced by it. In this map, these external (exogenous) variables appear in bold text around the edges of the map, with no incoming arrows. Examples include Political will and Short-term funding cycles. These are often beyond the control of those operating within the system.

Socioecological levels

The loops are organised into four levels, from the most personal to the most structural:

🧍 IndividualMotivation, skills, trust, personal experience
🤝 RelationshipFamily, carers, peer networks, digital champions
🏘️ CommunityLocal organisations, libraries, voluntary sector, place-based assets
🏛️ SocietyPolicy, commercial forces, infrastructure, design, regulation

🧭 What this map is — and isn't

At its heart, a systems map is a sense-making tool. It describes the system as it currently exists; it does not prescribe what must change. The next step will be to identify leverage points — places in the system where professionals, policy makers, businesses, and community leaders could intervene to make a difference. The survey questions below invite you to start thinking about this.
